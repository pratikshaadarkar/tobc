How to Get Your Music Placed with Labels Like Zee Music, Sa Re Ga Ma, and Times Music

T
TOBC Team June 18, 2026  ·  8 min read
Music Industry

Let's be honest about something most people in the Indian music industry don't say out loud.

Getting your song on Zee Music or Sa Re Ga Ma isn't just about talent. It's about access. It's about knowing how these systems work, who reviews what, what they actually look for, and what gets a submission thrown in the bin before anyone even hits play.

Most independent artists don't have that knowledge. And they spend months sending emails into the void wondering why nothing comes back. This guide is for those artists. Here's how music label placement actually works in India, and what you can do to genuinely increase your chances.

First, Understand What These Labels Actually Are

Zee Music, Sa Re Ga Ma, and Times Music aren't just distributors. They're full music publishing companies with their own YouTube channels, streaming presence, A&R teams, and content strategies.

When they sign a song, they're not just "uploading it." They're committing marketing resources, channel placement, and promotional push behind it. That means they're selective, not because they want to be gatekeepers, but because they have limited bandwidth and they protect what they promote.

So the question they're always asking isn't "is this a good song?" It's: "Does this song fit what we're already doing, and can we make it perform?"

What Do Label A&R Teams Actually Look For?

Before you submit anything, understand what the people listening are evaluating.

Production quality, No label wants to invest in something that sounds like it was recorded in a bathroom. Your mix and master need to sound platform-ready. Not necessarily expensive, but professional.

Commercial fit, Labels have genres, themes, and aesthetics they've built audiences around. Sa Re Ga Ma's YouTube audience expects a certain kind of music. Times Music leans heavily into film and regional. Sending the wrong genre to the wrong label wastes everyone's time.

Artist narrative, Who are you? What's your story? Do you have even a small existing audience on Instagram or YouTube? Labels aren't looking for superstars, they're looking for artists who show momentum.

The hook, This one's non-negotiable. Is there a moment in the first 30 seconds of your song that makes someone stop scrolling? If not, it's back to the drawing board before you submit anything.

The Reality of Cold Submissions

You can email. You can DM. You can find a general inquiry form on a label's website and fill it out with hope in your heart. And most of the time, nothing will happen.

Not because your music is bad. Because labels receive hundreds of submissions weekly and most go unread. The cold submission pipeline is real but brutally competitive.

"The artists who actually get placed are usually the ones who get warm introductions, through a producer the label already works with, a director who's shot content for them, or a management company with an existing relationship."

This is where working with a production house that already has those connections changes everything.

What You Should Prepare Before Any Label Conversation

Whether you're going through a production house or pursuing a submission independently, have this ready before any conversation starts.

  • A fully produced, mixed, and mastered track, not a demo, not a rough. A finished song.
  • A music video or at least a lyric video, labels think in visual content. A song alone is harder to pitch than a song with a visual.
  • Your social media numbers, Instagram followers, YouTube subscribers, average Reels views. Even modest numbers show you have a base.
  • Your press kit, a one-page document with your artist photo, bio, genre, existing releases, and contact info. Keep it clean and short.
  • Clarity on what you want, distribution only? Active promotion? YouTube premiere? Know what you're asking for before the meeting.

The Labels Are Not the Finish Line

Getting placed with a label is a milestone, not a destination.

The artists who sustain careers after label placement are the ones who keep creating, keep building audience, and treat the label release as one chapter in a longer story, not the whole story.

The best label relationships are partnerships. You bring consistent, quality music. They bring distribution, promotion, and audience. When both sides are delivering, careers are built.
