How to Brief a Production House: What to Send Before the First Meeting

T
TOBC Team June 2, 2026  ·  9 min read
Company Insights

Here's a scenario that plays out in the Indian production industry more often than anyone wants to admit.

A brand books a meeting with a production house. The meeting happens. Everyone is enthusiastic. The production house goes away, spends three days building a concept, puts together a detailed proposal, and then gets a response that says: "Actually, we've changed direction. Can you redo this completely?"

Three more rounds. Three more revisions. Nobody's happy. The timeline has slipped by six weeks before a single shot has been planned. This happens because the brief was bad, not dishonest, just incomplete.

"A good brief doesn't just save time. It determines the quality of everything that follows."

Here's what a brief for a production house should actually contain.

1. The Business Context (Not Just the Creative Ask)

Start with why this project exists, not what you want to make.

What business problem are you solving? Are you launching a new product? Trying to shift perception after a PR issue? Entering a new market? Launching a new brand identity?

A production house that understands your business context will make creative decisions that serve your actual goals, not just decisions that look good in a sizzle reel. Skip this, and you're asking a director to build something without knowing what it's supposed to hold.

2. Your Target Audience (With Actual Specificity)

"Young urban professionals aged 25-35" is not an audience. It's a demographic.

An audience is: "A 28-year-old woman in Bangalore who just got her first real salary hike, is upgrading her lifestyle, and is slightly overwhelmed by how many choices she has."

The more specifically you describe the person you're making this for, the better every creative decision becomes. The director can cast for her. The stylist can dress for her. The writer can speak to her. Vague audience = vague content.

3. The Single Most Important Message

Not three messages. Not five brand pillars. One message.

After someone watches this piece of content, what's the one thing you want them to walk away with? If you can't answer this in one sentence, your brief isn't ready yet. Come back when you can.

Production houses that accept complex briefs without pushing back on this are doing you a disservice. Every frame of a good film is in service of one central idea. Multiple messages create multiple films, and none of them do their job.

4. The Format and Duration

What exactly are you making? A 30-second TVC for broadcast? A 2-minute brand film for YouTube? A 15-second pre-roll cut for Instagram? A full content series? A music video?

The format shapes everything: crew size, shoot days, post-production timeline, and cost. Don't leave this to be "figured out later." Also mention if you need multiple cuts from the same shoot, a 60-second master with 30-second and 15-second cuts should be in the brief, not discovered in the final edit conversation.

5. Visual References

Don't describe the mood you want. Show it.

Pull 5-10 references, films, ads, photographs, color palettes, anything visual that says "this is the world we want to live in." No production house can perfectly read your taste from words like "premium" or "aspirational", those words mean different things to different people. References aren't constraints. They're communication tools.

6. Budget Range

Yes, you need to share this. The two most common reasons brands don't share budgets upfront: they're worried the production house will eat the entire amount, or they don't actually know what things cost and don't want to look uninformed.

Both are understandable. Neither helps you. A production house that doesn't know your budget will either over-pitch (building a concept you can't afford) or under-pitch (giving you something that doesn't match your ambition). Sharing a realistic range means you get proposals built for what's actually possible.

7. Timeline and Key Dates

Are there any dates this film absolutely cannot miss? A product launch event? A seasonal window? A board presentation? Fixed dates create real constraints that affect everything from crew booking to post-production turnaround. Share them upfront. A production house that knows your hard deadline can build a realistic backwards timeline, or tell you honestly if it isn't achievable.

8. Decision-Making Process

Who has final approval on this project? If the final call requires the CEO's sign-off and the CEO hasn't seen the brief yet, that's something the production house needs to know. Concepts that go through five approval layers before a decision is made need different timelines than projects where one person has authority. Be transparent about how decisions are made. It protects both sides.

Brief Checklist

Business context, the problem this project solves, target audience, specific person, not broad demographic, single key message, one sentence maximum, format and duration, including all cuts needed, visual references, 5 to 10 examples, budget range, be honest, it helps everyone, key dates, hard deadlines and ideal delivery, and decision-making process, who approves what.
